# Sources — Every Way Your Memory Lies to You

Memory isn't a recording — it's a reconstruction, rebuilt (and quietly edited) every
time you recall it. This video walks through the main ways memory distorts: why it's
fallible by design, and why that's mostly a feature, not a bug. Evidence-based,
fascinating, humble. Not a diagnosis.

## The core idea: memory is reconstructive, not a recording
- We imagine memory as a video camera that records and plays back the past. It isn't.
  **Memory is reconstructive** (Bartlett, 1932): each recall **rebuilds** the event from
  fragments, filling gaps with expectation, belief, and later information — and the
  rebuilt version can be **re-saved with the edits**. You don't retrieve the past; you
  **re-assemble** it.

## 1. The misinformation effect
- **Elizabeth Loftus:** information encountered **after** an event can rewrite the
  memory of it. In the classic study, people who were asked how fast cars were going
  when they **"smashed"** (vs "hit") recalled higher speeds and even **false broken
  glass**. Leading questions and after-the-fact details reshape what we "remember."

## 2. False memories — whole events that never happened
- Loftus's **"lost in the mall"** work: with suggestion, a meaningful share of people
  can be led to **confidently "remember"** entire childhood events that **never
  occurred**. Memory doesn't just distort details — it can **manufacture whole
  episodes**, vividly and sincerely.

## 3. Source confusion (misattribution)
- We often remember the **content** but misremember **where it came from** — mixing up
  whether something was seen, imagined, dreamed, or heard from someone else. This
  **source-monitoring error** is behind "I'm sure you told me that" and
  **cryptomnesia** (mistaking an old memory for an original idea).

## 4. The Mandela effect — shared false memories
- Large groups can **confidently share the same false memory** (e.g. a famous logo,
  line, or spelling misremembered the same way). It's usually explained by **schema and
  gist** — we remember the **meaning**, then reconstruct plausible-but-wrong details
  the same way others do.

## 5. Flashbulb memories feel vivid — and are often wrong
- "Flashbulb" memories of shocking events (where you were when you heard big news) feel
  **crystal clear and certain** — yet studies tracking them over years show they
  **drift and change** like any memory. **Confidence is not accuracy.** Vividness is a
  feeling, not proof.

## 6. The telephone / retelling effect
- Every time you **retell** a story, you can subtly reshape it — smoothing, dramatising,
  simplifying — and the **retold version** becomes the new memory. Memories you revisit
  most can end up the **least** faithful to what actually happened.

## 7. Bias of the present — rosy retrospection & hindsight
- The present **colours** the past. **Rosy retrospection** makes us remember past events
  as better than we rated them at the time; **hindsight bias** ("I knew it all along")
  rewrites our past predictions to match what happened. Your current mood and knowledge
  quietly **edit the archive**.

## 8. Imagination inflation & suggestibility
- Simply **imagining** an event can make it feel more like a real memory
  (**imagination inflation**). Memory is **suggestible** — which is why **eyewitness
  testimony**, despite feeling certain, is a leading contributor to wrongful
  convictions (per the Innocence Project), and why leading questions are so dangerous.

## Why memory is built this way (the reassurance)
- A perfect recording would be **overwhelming and useless**. Memory stores the **gist
  and meaning**, not every pixel, so it can **generalise, predict, and imagine
  futures**. The same flexible, reconstructive system that lets us **plan and create**
  is the one that makes us misremember. The bug **is** the feature.

## How to hold memory wisely (stated in the video)
- Hold your memories, especially vivid or emotional ones, a little **more loosely**.
- **Confidence isn't proof** — be willing to be wrong, even about things you're sure of.
- For things that matter, **write it down / keep a record** rather than trusting recall.
- Beware **leading questions** (in yourself and others); don't "remind" people into
  memories.
- **Not a diagnosis:** everyday distortion is normal and universal; significant,
  worsening memory loss is different and worth a doctor's visit.
