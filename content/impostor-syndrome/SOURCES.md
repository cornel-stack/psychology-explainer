# Sources — Impostor Syndrome: Why You Feel Like a Fraud

**Impostor syndrome** (more precisely the "impostor phenomenon") is the persistent
internal experience of believing you are not as capable as others think, and a
fear of being "found out" as a fraud — *despite* clear external evidence of
competence. First described by psychologists **Pauline Rose Clance and Suzanne
Imes (1978)**.

## Key facts
- **Extremely common:** an estimated ~70% of people experience it at some point
  (Gravois / review literature). Originally studied in high-achieving women; now
  recognised across genders, fields and achievement levels.
- **It targets the competent:** paradoxically, it tends to strike capable, often
  high-achieving people — those who care, who are learning, who are in new or
  stretching roles.
- **The core engine (attribution):** sufferers **discount their successes**
  (attributing them to luck, timing, charm, or error) and **internalise their
  failures** (seeing them as proof of true inadequacy). The evidence of competence
  never "lands."

## The impostor cycle (Clance)
Success → "I only fooled them / got lucky" → fear of being exposed → over-prepare
or self-sabotage → (another) success → the belief is never updated. Each win
*increases* the anxiety instead of relieving it.

## The five types (Clance)
1. **The Perfectionist** — sets impossibly high standards; 99% feels like failure.
2. **The Expert** — never feels they know enough; fears being exposed as
   inexperienced; endless credential-collecting.
3. **The Soloist** — believes asking for help proves they're a fraud; must do
   everything alone.
4. **The Natural Genius** — believes competence should be effortless; feels shame
   when something is hard or takes practice.
5. **The Superhero** — overworks and overextends to prove worth; ties identity to
   output.

## Why it feels true (the competence paradox)
- The more you learn, the more you can see how much you *don't* know — so genuine
  growth can increase the feeling of inadequacy (related to the Dunning–Kruger
  effect: experts underestimate themselves; novices overestimate). Confidence
  often lags real competence.

## Who gets it / context
- Not a personal failing or a diagnosis (not in the DSM). More common in new
  roles, high-pressure fields, and among people from **underrepresented groups**
  who lack others "like them" in the room. It's a response to context, not proof
  of fraudulence.

## How to loosen its grip (the fixes in the video)
- **Name it and normalise it** — simply knowing it's a common, recognised pattern
  (and that others feel it too) strips away much of its power; talk about it.
- **Separate feelings from facts** — "I feel like a fraud" is a feeling, not
  evidence. Weigh it against the actual record.
- **Collect the evidence** — keep a concrete list/file of wins, positive feedback,
  and accomplishments to counter the discounting.
- **Reframe success and failure** — own your wins as skill, not luck; treat
  mistakes as normal and temporary, not proof of fraudulence.
- **Accept you don't need to know everything** — competence includes learning;
  asking for help is strength, not exposure.

## Honest framing (stated in the video)
- Compassionate and normalising; "if you feel this, you're in extremely good (and
  very capable) company." Psycho-education, not therapy — if the fear is
  overwhelming or tied to anxiety/depression, professional support helps.
