# Sources — Why You Can Feel Someone Watching You

That prickle on the back of your neck, the certainty that someone is staring — then
you turn, and they are. Is it a sixth sense? This episode explains the real, well-
evidenced perceptual and social machinery behind the feeling, and gently debunks the
psychic version. Psycho-education, not diagnosis.

## Why we're so tuned to being watched
- **A dedicated gaze-detection system:** the human brain has specialised circuitry
  (notably the superior temporal sulcus, STS, plus amygdala) for detecting eyes and
  judging gaze direction. We spot direct gaze faster than averted gaze (the "stare-
  in-the-crowd" effect) and are remarkably accurate at telling whether someone is
  looking at us.
- **The cooperative-eye hypothesis** (Tomasello et al.): humans are the only primate
  with such visible white sclera, making the direction of our gaze easy to read. We
  evolved to broadcast and track where others are looking — so eyes grab our
  attention automatically.
- **Peripheral vision & motion detection:** the edges of your visual field are poor
  at detail but excellent at catching movement. A subtle head-turn, a shift of
  posture, a shadow — detected pre-consciously — can trigger the "someone's watching"
  feeling before you consciously know why, prompting you to turn and check.
- **Threat detection / the amygdala:** across the animal kingdom a direct stare is a
  threat and a potent social signal. Being looked at raises physiological arousal;
  the brain treats possible observation as something worth checking, fast — a
  better-safe-than-sorry bias.

## Why it feels like a sixth sense (and isn't)
- **Scopaesthesia / "the feeling of being stared at":** the popular belief that we
  can literally sense an unseen gaze. Studied since Titchener (1898), who attributed
  it to nervousness + turning-to-check behaviour. Modern controlled tests (Marks &
  Colwell; Wiseman) find no reliable evidence for a genuine extrasensory "staring
  detection." The experience is real; the psychic explanation is not.
- **The self-fulfilling turn:** when you feel watched and turn around, your own
  movement draws other people's eyes toward you — so they really are looking… because
  you just moved. The "confirmation" is partly created by the act of checking.
- **Confirmation bias / illusory correlation:** we vividly remember the hits ("I knew
  it — they were staring!") and quietly forget the misses (felt watched, no one
  there). This lopsided memory makes the sense feel far more accurate than chance.

## When the feeling changes behaviour
- **The spotlight effect** (Gilovich): we overestimate how much others notice and
  watch us — feeling observed even when we're not.
- **Audience effect / observation changes us** (classic social-facilitation and
  Hawthorne-type findings): believing we're watched raises self-consciousness and
  shifts performance and behaviour.
- **Hypervigilance:** anxiety, past threat, or heightened arousal turns the watch-
  detector up — over-detecting eyes and attention. (At the extreme, persistent,
  distressing certainty of being watched/followed can signal paranoia and deserves
  professional support — flagged in the video, not diagnosed.)

## How to settle it (gentle, stated in the video)
- Name it as the detector doing its job, not proof.
- Reality-check: actually look; notice the misses, not just the hits.
- Ground and down-regulate arousal (breath, context, safety cues).
- If the feeling is constant, distressing, or tips into certainty of being followed/
  surveilled, that's worth talking to a professional about. Not a diagnosis.
