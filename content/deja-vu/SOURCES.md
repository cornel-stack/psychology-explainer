# Sources — The Psychology of Déjà Vu

That uncanny jolt — "I've been here before," while knowing full well you haven't.
What déjà vu actually is, the leading theory of why it happens, and why it may be a
sign your memory is working *well*. Wondrous and evidence-based. Not diagnosis.

## What it is
- **Déjà vu** (French, "already seen"): the fleeting, eerie feeling that a *new*
  experience is a *repeat* of a past one — combined with the clear awareness that it
  can't actually be. The defining feature is that **conflict**: it feels familiar,
  yet you know it's genuinely new.

## Who gets it
- Very common: roughly **two-thirds of people** report experiencing it. It's reported
  **more by younger people** (peaks in teens/twenties) and declines with age, and
  more when **tired, stressed, or travelling** / in novel places.

## The leading explanation: a memory mismatch
- **Two memory processes** (dual-process models of recognition): *familiarity* (a
  vague sense of "I know this") and *recollection* (actually retrieving the specific
  where/when). Normally they fire together.
- **Déjà vu = familiarity without recollection:** a strong feeling of familiarity
  switches on, but recollection comes up empty — you feel you've been here, but can
  retrieve nothing, *and* you know it's new. That three-way clash is the uncanny
  signature.

## The "fact-checker" theory (it may mean your memory is healthy)
- **Akira O'Connor & colleagues:** brain-imaging suggests déjà vu involves the
  **frontal, decision-making regions** rather than just memory regions — as if the
  brain is **error-checking** a false signal of familiarity and flagging it:
  "something here is off." On this view, déjà vu is your memory system **catching a
  mistake** — a feature, not a glitch, and possibly why it declines with age as
  checking weakens.

## The layout / gestalt-familiarity trigger
- **Anne Cleary's lab:** a *new* scene that shares the **spatial layout** of a scene
  you've seen before (but don't consciously recall) can trigger déjà vu. In VR
  experiments, rooms arranged like a previously-seen (but forgotten) room reliably
  produced the feeling — familiarity from the forgotten layout, without the source
  memory.

## The neural seat
- **Temporal lobe:** déjà vu is linked to the **medial temporal lobe** (hippocampus,
  rhinal cortices). People with **temporal-lobe epilepsy** often experience intense
  déjà vu as a *seizure aura*, which helped pinpoint where in the brain the feeling
  is generated. A brief mis-timing or over-activation there may spark it.

## Jamais vu (the mirror image)
- **Jamais vu** ("never seen"): the opposite — a *familiar* thing suddenly feels
  **alien** (e.g. a common word looks wrong/misspelled after you stare at it).
  Thought to arise from the same familiarity machinery, running the other way.

## Honest framing (stated in the video)
- Ordinary déjà vu is **benign, common, and likely a sign of healthy memory-checking**
  — nothing to fear.
- Caveat (not a diagnosis): *frequent, intense* déjà vu, especially with other signs —
  zoning out, a rising odd feeling or smell, confusion, lost time — can be a temporal-
  lobe seizure and is worth a conversation with a doctor.
