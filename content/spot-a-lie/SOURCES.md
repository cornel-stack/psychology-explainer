# Sources — The Real Signs Someone Is Lying

An honest, myth-busting look at lie detection: why most popular "tells" are wrong,
why humans are barely better than chance, and what the evidence actually supports.
Framed responsibly — the goal is better thinking, not paranoid accusation. Psycho-
education, not a how-to for interrogating loved ones.

## We believe by default (and mostly should)
- **Truth-Default Theory** (Timothy Levine): humans operate on a default assumption
  that others are honest. It's efficient and usually correct — most communication is
  truthful — but it leaves us open to the occasional lie. This default is a feature,
  not a failing.

## We're bad at spotting lies
- **~54% accuracy** (Bond & DePaulo, 2006 meta-analysis of ~24,000 judges): average
  human lie-detection accuracy is only slightly above the 50% of a coin flip. Even
  "professionals" (police, judges) do little better. Confidence does not track
  accuracy.

## Most popular "tells" are myths
- **DePaulo et al. (2003) meta-analysis:** there is **no single reliable nonverbal
  cue to deception** — no Pinocchio's nose. Gaze aversion, fidgeting, touching the
  face/nose, looking "up and to the left," crossed arms: not reliably linked to
  lying. Many liars make *more* eye contact to seem convincing.
- **The Othello error** (Ekman): mistaking signs of *nervousness or stress* for signs
  of *lying*. Honest people under suspicion get nervous too, so "they looked anxious"
  is a terrible basis for calling someone a liar.
- **Microexpressions** (Paul Ekman): brief involuntary facial flashes are real, but
  they're fleeting, hard to read reliably, easily missed, and heavily overhyped by TV.
  Not a dependable everyday lie detector.

## What the evidence actually supports
- **Cognitive load / "lying is harder work"** (Aldert Vrij): fabricating and
  maintaining a false story, while monitoring the listener, takes more mental effort
  than recalling the truth. So load-based methods help more than watching body
  language.
- **Verbal content beats body language:** truthful accounts tend to be richer, more
  detailed, more spontaneous; lies are often thinner, vaguer, or over-rehearsed.
  Listen to *what* is said, not just how someone fidgets.
- **Impose load to reveal it** (Vrij): ask for more detail, ask unexpected questions,
  request the story in *reverse order*, ask to draw/sketch it — these increase the
  liar's load and surface inconsistencies far better than "read the eyes."
- **Baselines, clusters & change:** any cue is meaningless alone; what matters is a
  *cluster* of signals and a *change from that person's normal baseline* — and even
  then it signals stress/discomfort, not necessarily deception.

## Honest framing (stated in the video)
- You cannot reliably "catch a liar" from body language, and acting as if you can
  damages trust and harms innocent people (the Othello error cuts deep).
- The responsible move when you suspect a lie: stay curious not accusatory, gather
  information, seek corroboration, and weigh verbal content — not twitch-reading.
- Not for surveilling or interrogating partners/kids; chronic suspicion in a
  relationship is itself worth reflecting on, and support can help.
