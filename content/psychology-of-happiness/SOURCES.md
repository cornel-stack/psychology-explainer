# Sources — The Psychology of Happiness

What positive psychology and happiness research say about what actually makes us
happy — and why we so often chase it in the wrong places. Core sources: Sonja
Lyubomirsky (*The How of Happiness*), Daniel Kahneman, Ed Diener, Martin Seligman,
the Harvard Study of Adult Development, Tal Ben-Shahar, Mihály Csíkszentmihályi.

## The traps (why we chase it wrong)
- **The arrival fallacy** (Tal Ben-Shahar): the belief that reaching a goal ("when
  I get the job / house / body") will bring lasting happiness; the feeling fades
  fast and the goalpost moves.
- **Hedonic adaptation / the hedonic treadmill** (Brickman & Campbell): we quickly
  adapt to new circumstances, good or bad, and return toward a baseline. New things
  give a spike of joy that fades. (Famous lottery-winners vs accident-victims
  study.)
- **The happiness "set point" / the pie** (Lyubomirsky's popular, *debated/
  oversimplified* model): roughly ~50% genetic set point, ~10% life circumstances,
  ~40% intentional activity. We present it explicitly as a rough, contested model —
  the real, useful takeaway is that a meaningful chunk is within your control
  through what you do.
- **Money & happiness:** past the point of comfortable needs, more money yields
  steeply diminishing returns on day-to-day happiness (Easterlin paradox;
  Kahneman & Deaton's ~$75k plateau for emotional wellbeing — later work (Killingsworth)
  nuanced this, but the core point stands: income matters far less than we assume,
  and we badly overrate it).

## What actually works (the evidence-backed sources)
1. **Relationships** — the single strongest predictor. The 80+ year Harvard Study
   of Adult Development: "good relationships keep us happier and healthier" — the
   quality of close connections, not wealth or fame, best predicts a good life.
2. **Experiences over things** (Gilovich): experiential purchases bring more lasting
   happiness than material ones — they resist adaptation, build identity and
   connection, and get better in memory.
3. **Flow** (Csíkszentmihályi): deep, absorbed engagement in challenging activity;
   among the most reliably satisfying states.
4. **Gratitude** (Emmons & McCullough): regularly noting what you're grateful for
   measurably raises wellbeing; counters adaptation by re-noticing the good.
5. **Giving / kindness** (Dunn, Aknin & Norton): spending on others and acts of
   kindness reliably boost the giver's happiness ("helper's high"; prosocial
   spending).
6. **Presence / attention** (Killingsworth & Gilbert, "a wandering mind is an
   unhappy mind"): being present in the moment correlates with greater happiness;
   mind-wandering predicts lower mood.

## Happiness vs meaning
- **Hedonic vs eudaimonic wellbeing:** feeling good (pleasure/happiness) is
  different from a sense of meaning/purpose. Research (Baumeister et al.) finds
  they overlap but diverge; the richest lives aim for **both** — enjoyment *and*
  meaning.

## The paradox (and the close)
- **Chasing happiness directly can backfire** (Iris Mauss): valuing/pursuing
  happiness too intensely is linked to *lower* happiness and more loneliness.
  Happiness tends to arrive as a **byproduct** of meaningful engagement,
  connection and contribution — not as a target you grab at.

## Honest framing (stated in the video)
- The 50/10/40 pie is a rough, debated model — presented as a motivating heuristic,
  not hard fact; emphasis on the controllable portion.
- Uplifting and practical; small, repeatable actions over grand pursuits.
- Psycho-education, not therapy; persistent low mood or depression is different from
  ordinary unhappiness and deserves professional support.
