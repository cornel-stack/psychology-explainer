# Sources — The Psychology of First Impressions

How fast we judge, why those snap judgments stick, the biases that run them, and how
to make — and read — a first impression wisely. Evidence-based, warm, practical. Not a
diagnosis.

## They form astonishingly fast
- **Willis & Todorov (2006):** people form impressions of a face — trustworthiness,
  likeability, competence, aggressiveness, attractiveness — in about **one tenth of a
  second**. More exposure time didn't change the *judgment*; it only made people more
  **confident** in it. The snap verdict is already made before you consciously decide.

## Thin-slicing
- **Ambady & Rosenthal (1992):** judgments made from **tiny "thin slices"** of
  behaviour — a few seconds of silent video — predicted outcomes (like teacher
  evaluations) about as well as much longer observation. The brain extracts a
  surprising amount from very little, very fast.

## The primacy effect
- **Solomon Asch (1946):** the **order** of information shapes the whole impression.
  The same trait list — intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn,
  envious — produces a **warmer** impression than the **reverse** order. Early
  information acts as an anchor that colours how we read everything after it.

## The halo effect
- **Halo effect (Thorndike; Nisbett & Wilson, "the warm-cold" studies):** one positive
  trait **bleeds into** judgments of unrelated ones. Physically attractive people are
  rated as more kind, intelligent, trustworthy and competent — the "**what is
  beautiful is good**" stereotype (Dion, Berscheid & Walster, 1972).

## Warmth and competence — the two questions
- **Fiske, Cuddy & Glick (stereotype content model):** nearly all first impressions run
  on **two universal dimensions** — **warmth** (is this person friend or foe, do they
  mean me well?) and **competence** (can they act on it?). **Warmth is judged first**
  and weighted more heavily — evolutionarily, "friend or foe?" came before "strong or
  weak?"

## First impressions are sticky (and self-fulfilling)
- **Confirmation bias** makes us notice evidence that fits the first read and discount
  what doesn't. And via **behavioural confirmation / self-fulfilling prophecy**
  (Snyder, Tanke & Berscheid, 1977), we subtly treat people in line with our
  expectation — and they respond in kind, "confirming" it.

## The pratfall effect
- **Aronson, Willerman & Floyd (1966):** a highly **competent** person becomes **more**
  likeable after a small, humanising blunder (spilling coffee); the same blunder makes
  an average person *less* likeable. Minor imperfection, on a base of competence, reads
  as warmth.

## Managing and reading them well (stated in the video)
- **Lead with warmth**, not just competence — a genuine smile, open posture, eye
  contact, using someone's name.
- **Nonverbals dominate** the first seconds — posture, expression, vocal tone carry
  more than the words.
- **First impressions are fast but fallible** — hold yours loosely, and give people (and
  yourself) a **second look**. You *can* update an impression with consistent later
  behaviour; it just takes more evidence than the first one did.

## Honest caveat
- Not a diagnosis, and not a rule about any individual — snap judgments are often wrong
  and carry real bias. The useful move is **awareness**: know the shortcuts your mind is
  taking so you can choose when to trust them and when to look again.
