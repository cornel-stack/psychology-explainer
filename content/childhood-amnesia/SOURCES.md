# Sources — Why You Can't Remember Being a Baby (Childhood Amnesia)

You learned to walk, talk, and love in your first years — the most important learning
of your life — yet you can't recall a second of it. Why "childhood amnesia" happens,
and why your earliest memory is probably partly invented. Wondrous and evidence-based.
Not diagnosis.

## The phenomenon
- **Childhood (infantile) amnesia:** almost no adults can recall events from before
  about **age 3–3.5**, and memories stay sparse until around age 6–7. The boundary is
  the "offset." It's universal, and striking given how much we learn in those years.

## It's not that infants can't remember *at the time*
- Babies and toddlers clearly form and use memories (they recognise faces, learn
  words, imitate actions days later). The puzzle is that these early episodic memories
  **don't persist** into later childhood and adulthood — they're made, then lost.

## Why the memories don't last
- **An immature hippocampus:** the hippocampus, which binds the pieces of an episodic
  memory into a storable, retrievable whole, is still **developing** in infancy and
  early childhood. Early memories may never be encoded in a durable, adult-retrievable
  form.
- **Neurogenesis overwrites early memories** (Josselyn & Frankland; Akers et al.,
  2014): the infant hippocampus produces huge numbers of **new neurons**, and this
  rapid turnover appears to **disrupt and overwrite** existing memory circuits —
  high neurogenesis literally promotes forgetting of early memories (shown in rodents;
  a leading mechanism for the human offset).
- **No language, no narrative** (Katherine Nelson; Fivush): before fluent language and
  the ability to tell experiences as stories, events aren't encoded as retrievable
  **autobiographical narratives**. Children remember more, and earlier, when parents
  **reminisce elaborately** with them ("remember when we…?"), which scaffolds memory.
- **No stable self yet:** autobiographical memory requires a **concept of "me"** that
  events happen *to*. A sense of self emerges around 18–24 months (mirror
  self-recognition) and matures afterward; before it, experiences lack the "this
  happened to me" anchor.

## Your earliest memory is probably part-invention
- Many "earliest memories" are **reconstructed**, not genuinely recalled — assembled
  from **family photos, home videos, and repeated stories**, then felt as memory.
  Tell-tale signs: it's fuzzy, fragmentary, and often seen from a **third-person /
  outside view** (impossible for a true first-person memory). Studies find a large
  share of very early "memories" are likely fictional.

## Culture and individual differences
- The offset **varies by culture and upbringing**: cultures and families that
  emphasise elaborate storytelling and reminiscing tend to have **earlier** first
  memories; more independence-focused contexts, later. Small gender differences too.

## Why it matters, and the honest frame (stated in the video)
- Childhood amnesia is **normal and universal** — not a sign anything is wrong.
- Even though you can't consciously recall it, those early years still shaped you:
  **implicit** learning, attachment, and emotional patterns formed then persist
  without conscious memory.
- Not a diagnosis. (A note of care: this is also why very early adversity isn't
  consciously remembered yet can still leave marks; support helps when the past
  weighs on the present.)
