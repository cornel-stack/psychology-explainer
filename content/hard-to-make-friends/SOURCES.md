# Sources — The Psychology of People Who Struggle to Make Friends

Why making friends as an adult is genuinely hard — and why struggling with it is
usually about circumstances and hidden psychology, not a personal defect. Warm,
practical, destigmatizing. Psycho-education, not diagnosis.

## It's structurally hard (not just you)
- **Friendship needs repeated, unplanned interaction + proximity** (Rawlins; the
  classic "friendship factory" of school/college). As adults we lose the built-in
  pool of near-daily, low-stakes contact, so friendships stop forming by accident.
- **Time cost** (Jeffrey Hall, 2018): it takes roughly ~50 hours together to move
  from acquaintance to casual friend, ~90 hours to friend, and ~200+ hours to a
  close friend. Adults rarely have that many spare, shared hours — so it feels slow.
- **Mere exposure / proximity effect** (Zajonc; Festinger's Westgate study): we come
  to like people (and they us) largely through repeated contact. No repeated contact,
  no friendship — it's logistics as much as chemistry.
- **Life transitions shrink networks:** moving city, remote work, graduating,
  parenthood, and simply getting busier all cut the casual contact that grows
  friends. The problem is the situation, not the person.

## The hidden psychology that keeps us stuck
- **The liking gap** (Boothby, Clark & colleagues, 2018): after conversations, people
  consistently underestimate how much the other person liked them and enjoyed their
  company. We walk away feeling we flopped when we didn't — so we don't follow up.
- **Pluralistic ignorance / the approach standoff:** everyone waits to be approached,
  each assuming the others aren't interested or are already "sorted." The result is a
  room full of people who'd all like to connect, all holding back.
- **Social anxiety & the spotlight/transparency illusion:** we overestimate how
  harshly others judge us and how visible our nerves are, which makes initiating feel
  more dangerous than it is.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy:** expecting rejection leads to closed, guarded
  behaviour (less eye contact, less warmth), which really does cool others toward us —
  "confirming" the fear we started with.

## What actually builds friendship
- **Gradual, reciprocal self-disclosure** (Altman & Taylor's social penetration;
  Aron's "36 questions"): closeness grows as two people trade increasing vulnerability
  over time. Staying only on safe small-talk keeps friendships from deepening.
- **Initiative and follow-up:** friendships are disproportionately started and kept
  alive by the person willing to make the first move and the next plan. Consistency
  beats charisma.
- **Quality over quantity:** wellbeing tracks having a few close, dependable bonds,
  not a large network. One or two real friends is a genuine, sufficient win. Introversion
  is not the barrier; lack of repeated contact and initiation is.

## When it's worth extra support
- Persistent, intense fear of social situations that you avoid and that impairs your
  life can be **social anxiety disorder**, which is common and very treatable (e.g.
  CBT). Loneliness that's heavy and lasting also deserves care. Flagged in the video,
  not diagnosed — reaching out helps.
