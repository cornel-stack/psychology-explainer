# Sources — The Psychology of Gratitude

What gratitude really is, why it's one of the most reliable paths to wellbeing, the
brain and attention science behind it, how to practise it, and why it's not toxic
positivity. Warm, evidence-based, honest. Not a cure or a diagnosis.

## What gratitude is
- Gratitude is **noticing and appreciating the good** in your life — and often
  recognising that much of it comes from **outside yourself** (people, luck, the world).
  It's both a **momentary feeling** and a **trainable practice/disposition**, not just a
  fixed personality trait.

## It's one of the best-studied paths to wellbeing
- **Robert Emmons & Michael McCullough (2003):** people who kept a weekly **gratitude
  journal** were happier, more optimistic, and even exercised more than those who listed
  hassles. **Martin Seligman's "Three Good Things"** and the **gratitude letter/visit**
  reliably **raise happiness and lower depressive symptoms**, sometimes for months.

## Why it works — 1: it beats the negativity bias
- The brain has a **negativity bias**: bad events grab attention and stick harder than
  good ones (evolutionarily, missing a threat was costlier than missing a treat).
  Gratitude is a deliberate counterweight — it **turns attention back toward the good**
  that the bias would otherwise skip straight past.

## Why it works — 2: it fights hedonic adaptation
- We **adapt** to good things and stop noticing them (the new phone, the relationship,
  good health all fade into background). Gratitude **re-notices** — it makes the
  familiar vivid again, so you get to **feel** the good you already have instead of
  taking it for granted.

## Why it works — 3: attention and the brain
- Gratitude **shifts the lens from scarcity to abundance** — from what's missing to
  what's present. Neuroimaging links gratitude to activity in **reward and prefrontal
  regions** and the brain's feel-good and bonding chemistry; expressing it is tied to
  **oxytocin** and social closeness. (Effects are real but modest — gratitude is a
  practice, not a miracle.)

## It strengthens relationships
- **Sara Algoe's "find-remind-bind" theory:** expressing gratitude **finds** good
  partners, **reminds** us of their value, and **binds** us closer. Saying thank you —
  and meaning it — is a powerful, underused relationship glue; feeling appreciated makes
  people more invested and generous in return.

## Gratitude is a skill you can train
- It's not about being born sunny. Like a muscle, gratitude **strengthens with
  practice**. The more you look for things to appreciate, the more naturally your
  attention starts finding them — you're literally training what your mind notices.

## How to practise it (stated in the video)
- **Keep a gratitude journal** — and be **specific** ("the way a friend called to
  check in" beats "my friends"). Specificity is what makes it work.
- **Savour** good moments as they happen — slow down and actually feel them.
- **Write a gratitude letter** to someone who shaped you; delivering it in person
  ("the gratitude visit") is one of the most powerful happiness exercises in psychology.
- **Mental subtraction:** imagine a good thing **absent** from your life — it snaps you
  out of taking it for granted (the "It's a Wonderful Life" effect).
- **Thank people out loud**, often and sincerely.

## Honest caveat — not toxic positivity
- Gratitude is **not** denial, suppression, or "just be grateful" dismissal of real
  pain. Forced, defensive gratitude ("at least…") can be **toxic positivity**. Healthy
  gratitude **sits beside** grief, anger, and struggle — both can be true at once. It is
  **not a substitute** for treating depression or injustice. Not a diagnosis; if low
  mood persists, please seek real support. Done honestly, though, gratitude is one of
  the simplest, most reliable ways to feel more alive to the life you already have.
