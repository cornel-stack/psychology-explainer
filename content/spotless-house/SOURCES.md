# Sources — The Psychology of People Who Keep Their House Spotless

Why some people keep an immaculate home — the psychological functions tidiness
serves, the real benefits, and where healthy order tips into something harder.
Curious and compassionate, not mocking. Psycho-education, not diagnosis.

## It's rarely "just being tidy"
- Keeping a spotless space often serves deeper psychological functions: control,
  mood regulation, identity, and focus. It's a behaviour with reasons, not a quirk.

## Control and anxiety
- **A controllable domain:** in an uncertain, chaotic world, your home is one space
  you can actually order. Tidying restores a sense of control and predictability,
  which lowers anxiety (an internal locus of control over at least one corner of
  life). For many, "when everything feels out of control, I clean."
- **Cleaning as mood/stress regulation:** repetitive, structured cleaning can be
  calming and almost meditative, giving a hit of accomplishment and order. People
  often clean to discharge anxiety or regain composure after stress.

## Trait and sensitivity differences
- **Conscientiousness / orderliness** (Big Five): orderliness is a facet of
  conscientiousness — a partly heritable, stable trait. High-orderliness people
  genuinely feel better in tidy environments; it isn't an affectation.
- **Disgust sensitivity:** people vary a lot in how strongly they react to mess,
  germs and "ick." Higher disgust sensitivity (an evolved pathogen-avoidance system)
  drives more cleaning. Individual dials differ.

## Real cognitive and physiological effects
- **Clutter taxes attention** (McMains & Kastner, Princeton): visual clutter competes
  for neural processing and reduces the ability to focus; clearing it can improve
  concentration.
- **Clutter and stress** (Saxbe & Repetti, UCLA): people who described their homes as
  cluttered/unfinished showed flatter, less healthy daily cortisol patterns, linking
  messy-feeling homes to higher stress. (Correlational — framed carefully.)
- A tidy, ordered environment can support calm, focus and even healthier choices for
  many people. Benefits are real but individual; some people thrive in creative
  "mess," and that's fine too.

## Where it tips over (the honest caveat)
- There's a spectrum. For most, keeping a clean home is a **helpful habit** that
  serves them. It becomes a problem when it starts to **control them**: when cleaning
  is driven by intrusive fears (contamination, something-bad-will-happen), must be
  done in a rigid "just right" way, eats hours, causes distress, or damages
  relationships and relaxation. That can point to **OCD** (contamination or symmetry
  subtypes), anxiety, or perfectionism — all common and treatable.
- Useful question: does the cleaning serve you, or do you serve it? Can you leave a
  dish overnight without distress?
- Not a diagnosis. Persistent, distressing, time-consuming cleaning compulsions
  deserve a conversation with a professional; effective help (e.g. CBT/ERP) exists.
