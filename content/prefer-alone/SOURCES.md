# Sources — The Psychology of People Who Prefer Being Alone

A warm, affirming trait-portrait of people who genuinely like their own company —
distinguishing healthy **solitude** from painful **loneliness**, and introversion
from shyness or antisocial avoidance.

## Key ideas
- **Introversion & the social battery:** Jung's introversion/extraversion;
  Eysenck's arousal theory — introverts have higher baseline cortical arousal, so
  busy social settings over-stimulate and *drain* them, while quiet recharges.
  Not shyness (fear) and not disliking people — a different energy economy.
- **Solitude ≠ loneliness:** loneliness is the painful *gap* between the connection
  you want and have; solitude is *chosen* aloneness that restores. (Research on
  the benefits of solitude — e.g. Thuy-vy Nguyen; the "solitude paradox.")
- **The restorative power of alone time:** chosen solitude supports emotion
  regulation, creativity, self-reflection and autonomy (self-determination
  theory — the need for autonomy).
- **Quality over quantity in relationships:** people who prefer solitude often
  invest in a few deep bonds rather than many shallow ones — consistent with
  research that close-tie quality predicts wellbeing more than sheer network size.
- **Rich inner life:** correlations between introversion/solitude-preference and
  reflective, imaginative inner activity; time alone as the workshop of
  creativity (many creatives protected solitude deliberately).
- **Self-awareness & a stable self-concept:** reflective solitude is linked with
  knowing one's values and a steadier sense of self; less dependence on external
  validation.
- **The observer stance:** a tendency to watch and listen rather than perform; can
  heighten perceptiveness.
- **Nature & walking:** solitary time in nature is especially restorative
  (attention-restoration theory — Kaplan).

## The nine traits (as presented)
1. They **recharge in solitude** (the social battery).
2. They keep **a few deep friendships**, not many shallow ones.
3. They're **fiercely independent** — comfortable doing things solo.
4. They have a **rich inner world** — imagination, reflection, ideas.
5. They **guard their energy** — strong, quiet boundaries.
6. They're **at ease with silence** — no need to fill it.
7. They're **highly observant** — they watch and notice.
8. They have a **strong sense of self** — they know who they are.
9. They **come back fuller** — alone time is their strength, not a deficit.

## Honest framing (stated in the video)
- Explicit and repeated: preferring solitude is **healthy and normal**, not a
  disorder, not "antisocial," and not the same as being lonely or shy.
- One gentle caveat: solitude is restorative when *chosen*; if aloneness is really
  avoidance driven by anxiety or low mood, that's worth tending — framed with care,
  not alarm, and "not a diagnosis."
- Dignity-first throughout; the tone celebrates the trait while respecting people
  who feel differently.
