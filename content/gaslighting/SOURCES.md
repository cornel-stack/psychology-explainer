# Sources — The Psychology of Gaslighting

What gaslighting actually is (a form of emotional abuse, not an ordinary disagreement),
the tactics, why it works on even the smartest people, what it does to you, and how to
find your way back to solid ground. Compassionate, careful, evidence-informed. Not a
diagnosis — and if you recognise this, it is not your fault, and support exists.

## What it is
- **Gaslighting is a form of psychological manipulation** in which a person tries to
  make someone **doubt their own memory, perception, judgment, or sanity**, in order to
  gain **power and control**. The goal isn't to win a point — it's to make you
  **distrust your own mind** and rely on the manipulator's version of reality.
- The term comes from the 1938 play **"Gas Light"** (and the 1944 film): a husband
  secretly **dims the gas lamps**, then insists to his wife that **nothing has
  changed** and she's imagining it — slowly convincing her she's losing her grip.

## It's a gradual process, not one lie
- Gaslighting works **over time**. It's not a single falsehood but a **slow, repeated
  erosion** of your confidence in your own reality, until you start pre-emptively
  **doubting yourself** and **deferring** to them.

## The common tactics (stated in the video)
- **Denial:** flatly insisting events never happened — "That never happened," "I never
  said that," even when you clearly remember it.
- **Countering:** attacking your **memory** — "You always remember things wrong."
- **Trivialising:** shrinking your feelings — "You're overreacting," "You're too
  sensitive," "Can't you take a joke?"
- **Blame-shifting / diverting:** turning it around so **you** end up apologising — "This
  is your fault," "You're the one with the problem."
- **Withholding / stonewalling:** refusing to engage — "I don't have time for this," "I
  don't know what you're talking about."

## Why it works — even on smart, grounded people
- We're wired to **trust people close to us**, so we give their version weight.
- **Repetition** wears down certainty; **cognitive dissonance** makes us resolve the
  gap by doubting ourselves rather than the person we love.
- **Isolation** removes the outside voices that could reality-check us.
- **Intermittent reinforcement:** occasional warmth and affection between the cold
  spells creates a powerful **trauma bond** and keeps us hooked, hoping the "good" them
  will return. Intelligence is **no protection** — this targets trust, not IQ.

## What it does to you
- Chronic **self-doubt**, confusion, anxiety; constantly **second-guessing** yourself;
  **apologising** all the time; "walking on eggshells"; feeling you've become **"too
  sensitive"** or that something is wrong with you; withdrawing from friends; losing
  your sense of who you are.

## Gaslighting vs. an ordinary disagreement
- A healthy disagreement **respects your reality** even while differing — "I remember it
  differently, let's figure it out." Gaslighting **attacks your grip on reality** — the
  aim is to make you wrong **about your own mind**. The word is now overused; **not
  every** denial or disagreement is gaslighting. The marker is a **sustained pattern**
  aimed at control.

## Finding your way back (stated in the video)
- **Trust your perception** — your feelings and memories are data, not defects.
- **Keep an external anchor:** a journal, texts, a record of what actually happened, so
  your reality doesn't depend on their account.
- **Break the isolation:** talk to trusted friends, family, or a professional who can
  reflect reality back to you.
- **Stop trying to "win"** the argument; you can't out-argue someone whose goal is your
  confusion. **Set boundaries**, and recognise that sometimes the healthiest answer is
  to **step back or leave**.

## Honest, important caveat
- This is **not a diagnosis**, and naming a dynamic isn't the same as labelling a
  person. But **genuine, sustained gaslighting is a serious form of emotional abuse.**
  If this pattern feels familiar, **it is not your fault**, you are **not** "too
  sensitive," and you deserve support — a trusted person, a therapist, or a domestic-
  abuse helpline. You are not crazy, and you are not alone.
