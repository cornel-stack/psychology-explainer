# Sources — Why You Remember Faces But Not Names

Why a face is instantly familiar but the name vanishes — the brain science of faces vs
names, the famous "Baker/baker paradox," and how to actually remember names. Light,
fascinating, evidence-based. Not a diagnosis.

## The universal experience
- Almost everyone knows the feeling: you recognise a face instantly — you *know* you
  know them — but the **name is simply gone.** This is normal and near-universal, not a
  sign of a failing memory. Faces and names are handled by **different systems**, and
  one is built for recognition while the other depends on fragile recall.

## Faces get dedicated brain machinery
- The brain has specialised hardware for faces: the **fusiform face area (FFA)**. We
  process faces **holistically** (as a whole pattern, not feature by feature), which is
  fast, rich, and deeply wired. Evolutionarily, reading faces — **friend, foe, kin,
  mood** — was survival-critical for hundreds of thousands of years.

## Names are arbitrary and meaningless
- A name is a **purely arbitrary verbal label.** "Rob" tells you nothing about the
  person — it connects to no features, no meaning, no web of associations. Faces are
  **meaningful patterns**; names are **abstract tags** the brain never evolved to store.
  Names are also a very **recent cultural invention** compared with face recognition.

## The Baker/baker paradox
- A classic demonstration: show people a face and tell half that the man's **name is
  Mr Baker**, and half that his **occupation is a baker**. People remember the
  **occupation far better than the identical word as a name.** Why? "Baker" the job
  links to a **rich web** — bread, ovens, aprons, a floury hat. "Baker" the name links
  to **nothing**. More hooks means easier retrieval. (McWeeny et al., 1987.)

## Recognition vs. recall
- Seeing a face is **recognition** — the easiest form of memory; the face itself is the
  cue. Producing a name is **recall** — you must generate it from scratch with **few or
  no cues.** Recall is far harder and more failure-prone, which is why names sit on the
  **"tip of the tongue."**

## Depth of processing — and not paying attention
- We process faces **deeply** (expression, features, character) and names **shallowly**.
  Worse, at the moment of introduction we're often **not even listening** — we're busy
  being nervous, planning what to say, or making an impression — so the name is **never
  properly encoded in the first place.** You can't recall what you never stored.

## Face blindness — the opposite extreme
- A small aside: some people have **prosopagnosia** ("face blindness") and genuinely
  **can't recognise faces**, even of close family — showing just how specialised, and
  separable, the face system is.

## How to remember names (stated in the video)
- **Actually pay attention** at the introduction and **repeat the name back** ("Nice to
  meet you, Sara").
- **Make it meaningful:** link the name to an image, a rhyme, or someone you know with
  that name — give it the hooks it lacks.
- **Attach it to the face:** connect the name to a distinctive feature.
- **Use it** in the conversation, and again when you say goodbye — **spacing** the
  retrieval locks it in.

## Honest caveat
- Forgetting names is overwhelmingly **normal** and gets a little more common with age.
  **Not a diagnosis.** But a **marked, worsening** decline in memory — forgetting
  familiar people, words, or recent events — is worth discussing with a doctor. Everyday
  name-blanking, though, is just your memory working exactly as it's built to.
