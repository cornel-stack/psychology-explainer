# Sources — Every Psychological Experiment Explained

The landmark experiments that shaped psychology — what each one did, what it revealed,
and the ethical reckoning several of them forced. Educational, honest, humble about
the dark ones. Not glorifying harm; several of these would never be allowed today.

## The through-line: the power of the situation
- A recurring lesson across these studies is **situationism**: ordinary people's
  behaviour is shaped far more by the **situation** they're in than we'd like to
  believe. Again and again, "good" people did troubling things under the right
  pressures — a humbling, important finding.

## 1. Pavlov's dogs — classical conditioning (1890s–1900s)
- **Ivan Pavlov:** dogs learned to **salivate at a bell** that had been repeatedly
  paired with food. A neutral stimulus, through association, triggers an automatic
  response. Foundation of **classical conditioning** — how we learn involuntary,
  reflexive associations.

## 2. Little Albert — conditioned fear (1920, Watson & Rayner)
- **John Watson:** conditioned an infant ("Little Albert") to **fear a white rat** by
  pairing it with a loud, frightening noise; the fear **generalised** to other furry
  things. Showed emotions can be conditioned — but it is **deeply unethical** (no
  consent, a distressed baby, never deconditioned) and could never be done today.

## 3. Milgram's obedience study (1961, Stanley Milgram)
- Participants were ordered by an authority figure to deliver what they believed were
  **painful, escalating electric shocks** to a stranger. A disturbing **~65% continued
  to the maximum 450 volts.** Revealed how far ordinary people will go when an
  **authority** takes responsibility — hugely influential, and **ethically fraught**
  (extreme stress, deception).

## 4. Asch conformity experiments (1950s, Solomon Asch)
- People judged which line matched a reference line — easy — but **confederates** gave
  obviously wrong answers aloud first. About **75% of participants conformed to the
  wrong answer at least once.** Showed the powerful pull of **group conformity**, even
  against the evidence of our own eyes.

## 5. Stanford Prison Experiment (1971, Philip Zimbardo)
- Students randomly assigned to be **"guards" or "prisoners"** in a mock prison; it
  escalated so badly it was **halted after 6 days**. Long cited as proof that
  **roles/situations** corrupt — though later **heavily criticised** (demand
  characteristics, coached guards, small sample). We present it **with** that critique:
  a cautionary tale about methods as much as human nature.

## 6. The bystander effect (1968, Darley & Latané)
- Sparked by the **Kitty Genovese** case narrative, experiments showed the **more
  people present, the less likely any one person is to help** — via **diffusion of
  responsibility** ("someone else will"). (Note: the original 38-witnesses news story
  was later shown to be exaggerated; the lab effect itself is robust.)

## 7. The Bobo doll experiment (1961, Albert Bandura)
- Children who watched an adult **hit an inflatable Bobo doll** were far more likely to
  **imitate that aggression** themselves. Foundation of **social learning theory**: we
  learn behaviour, including aggression, by **observing and modelling** others.

## 8. Harlow's monkeys — contact comfort (1950s–60s, Harry Harlow)
- Infant monkeys preferred a soft **cloth "mother"** over a wire one that provided
  food, clinging to it for **comfort and security**. Showed attachment is about
  **comfort and affection**, not just feeding — pivotal for attachment theory, but the
  **maternal-deprivation** work was **cruel** by modern standards.

## 9. The marshmallow test — delayed gratification (1972, Walter Mischel)
- Children could eat **one marshmallow now or wait for two.** Early follow-ups linked
  waiting to later outcomes — but **large replications** found the effect is **much
  smaller** and heavily tied to a child's **environment and trust/affluence** than
  "willpower." We present the **nuance**, not the myth.

## 10. The invisible gorilla — selective attention (1999, Simons & Chabris)
- Viewers counting basketball passes **failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit**
  walking through the scene. Demonstrates **inattentional blindness** — we miss
  obvious things when our attention is elsewhere; we see far less than we think.

## Honest framing (stated in the video)
- Several of these studies caused **real harm** and **could not be run today** — they
  helped build modern **ethics rules (informed consent, debriefing, review boards)**,
  often *because* of how badly they went. We study them for what they taught us about
  the mind **and** about how carefully science must be done. **Not** an endorsement of
  the methods. Replication matters: some famous findings are **weaker or messier** than
  the pop-culture version. Not a diagnosis; a tour of ideas, held with humility.
