# Sources — Why You Talk to Yourself

Why you narrate your day, mutter while searching for your keys, or hype yourself up
in the mirror — and why talking to yourself is not a sign of madness but one of the
mind's best tools. Reassuring, practical, evidence-based. Psycho-education, not
diagnosis.

## It's normal (and useful)
- Talking to yourself — out loud or silently as **inner speech** — is extremely
  common and healthy. It's a cognitive tool, not a red flag.

## Where it comes from
- **Private speech → inner speech** (Lev Vygotsky): young children talk themselves
  through tasks out loud ("now the red block goes here"). This "private speech" helps
  them self-guide, and over childhood it goes underground, becoming silent **inner
  speech** — a core instrument for thinking, planning and self-control. Out-loud
  self-talk is that same tool, surfacing again when a task is hard.

## What it does for you
- **Self-regulation & focus:** inner and out-loud speech help organise thoughts,
  hold a plan in mind, direct attention, and work through problems step by step
  ("rubber-duck debugging" — explaining a problem aloud often solves it).
- **It literally sharpens perception:** saying a target's name out loud speeds how
  fast you find it (Lupyan & Swingley, 2012) — talking to yourself while searching
  for the ketchup genuinely helps.
- **Motivation & performance:** instructional and motivational self-talk reliably
  improve task and athletic performance (Hatzigeorgiadis et al. meta-analysis; Hardy).
  Athletes, performers and surgeons use it deliberately.

## The clever tricks
- **Distanced / third-person self-talk** (Ethan Kross, *Chatter*): addressing
  yourself by name or as "you" ("Come on, Sam, you've got this") instead of "I"
  creates psychological distance, lowers anxiety, and improves performance and
  wisdom under stress. A tiny linguistic shift, a real effect.
- **Inner critic vs inner coach:** the *content and tone* of self-talk matter hugely.
  Harsh, critical self-talk feeds anxiety and low mood; kind, encouraging,
  coach-style self-talk (and self-compassion) builds resilience and calm.

## Not everyone narrates the same
- **Inner-speech varies widely:** some people have a near-constant inner monologue;
  others have little or none (recently termed **anendophasia**). Both are normal —
  having a quiet inner world doesn't mean you think less, just differently (more in
  images, sensations, or abstract thought).

## Honest framing (stated in the video)
- Talking to yourself is benign and beneficial — the stigma is unearned.
- Distinction (handled gently, not as diagnosis): ordinary self-talk is *you*
  talking. Hearing voices experienced as coming from *outside* or from someone
  *other* than yourself, especially if distressing or commanding, is a different
  thing and worth talking to a professional about. Support exists and helps.
