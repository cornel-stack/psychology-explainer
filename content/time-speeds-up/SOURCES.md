# Sources — Why Time Speeds Up As You Age

Why a childhood summer felt endless and now whole years vanish in a blink — the
psychology of time perception, and how to actually slow it back down. Wondrous,
practical, evidence-based. Not diagnosis.

## The phenomenon
- Almost universal: subjective time seems to **accelerate with age**. Children
  experience time as slow and expansive; adults feel it speed up ("where did the year
  go?"). It's one of the most relatable illusions of the mind.

## The proportional theory (the ratio)
- **Paul Janet (1897):** each interval of time is judged **relative to the total life
  lived so far**. To a 5-year-old, one year is 1/5 of their whole life — enormous. To
  a 50-year-old, a year is 1/50 — a sliver. So each successive year feels
  **proportionally shorter**. (A neat, if simplified, account of the long-run trend.)

## Memory density — we judge the past by how much we remember
- The dominant modern view: **retrospective time is judged by memory density** — how
  many distinct, new memories a period laid down. **Childhood is full of firsts**
  (first day of school, first bike, first everything), creating **rich, dense
  memories**, so that period *feels long* looking back. Adult life, full of **routine**
  and repetition, lays down **few new memories**, so time **compresses** in memory.

## The "holiday paradox" (Claudia Hammond)
- Time estimation differs **prospectively vs retrospectively**. A novel holiday can
  feel like it's **flying by in the moment** yet seem **long in hindsight** (packed
  with new memories). A dull routine week feels **slow in the moment** but **vanishes
  in retrospect** (nothing to remember). Novelty stretches remembered time; routine
  shrinks it.

## Time in the moment (prospective)
- **Attention matters:** when you watch the clock or are bored, time **drags**; when
  absorbed/busy, it **flies** ("time flies when you're having fun"). Few memory cues
  make a boring stretch feel endless *now* but short *later*.
- **Fear/arousal can slow perceived time** (adrenaline → denser encoding; "slow
  motion" in accidents — Eagleman shows it's a memory trick, not real slow-mo). The
  **oddball effect:** a novel/surprising stimulus seems to last longer.

## Possible biological angles (mentioned lightly, flagged as debated)
- Some propose the internal "pacemaker" slows with age (dopamine, metabolism, body
  temperature affect time estimates). **Adrian Bejan** argues older brains process
  images more slowly, so fewer mental "frames" per unit time → time feels faster.
  These are **contested**; the memory/novelty account is the best-supported.

## How to slow time down (stated in the video)
- **Seek novelty and break routine:** travel, learn new skills, take new routes, meet
  new people, change your environment — fresh experiences lay down more memories, so
  time feels fuller and longer.
- **Be present / savour:** mindfulness and attention to the moment thicken experience;
  autopilot thins it.
- **Mark time:** journaling, photos, celebrating milestones create memory anchors.
- The deeper point: a life rich in novelty and presence is not just slower — it's
  **fuller**. Not a diagnosis; simply a kinder way to spend your days.
