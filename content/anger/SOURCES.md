# Sources — The Psychology of Anger

Why we snap, what anger is really trying to tell us, why "venting" makes it worse,
and how to handle one of our most powerful, misunderstood emotions. Compassionate,
practical, evidence-based. Not diagnosis.

## Anger is normal — and a messenger
- Anger is a **universal, healthy emotion** — a response to **perceived threat,
  injustice, a blocked goal, or a crossed boundary**. In itself it is **not bad**; it
  carries information ("something here matters / isn't right") and **energy to act**.
  The problem is never the feeling — it's what we *do* with it.

## It's often a secondary emotion
- Anger frequently sits **on top of a softer, more vulnerable feeling** — hurt, fear,
  shame, embarrassment, powerlessness, rejection. Anger feels **powerful and
  protective**; the feelings beneath feel weak, so the mind reaches for anger as
  armour. (The "anger iceberg": anger is the visible tip; the real feeling is below.)

## The amygdala hijack
- **Daniel Goleman's "amygdala hijack":** the amygdala can trigger a fast fight-or-
  flight threat response — flooding the body with **adrenaline and cortisol** —
  *before* the rational **prefrontal cortex** can weigh in. In that flood, clear
  thinking is literally offline, which is why we do and say things we regret.

## The 90-second rule
- **Jill Bolte Taylor:** the **physiological surge** of an emotion — the chemical
  flood of anger through the body — runs its course in roughly **90 seconds**. After
  that, staying angry requires **re-triggering the thought** that feeds it. If you
  can ride out the first wave without acting, the chemistry subsides and choice
  returns.

## The venting myth
- **Catharsis theory is largely false** (Bushman, 2002; and others): "letting it out"
  — punching pillows, screaming, ranting, aggressive venting — does **not** discharge
  anger. It **rehearses and strengthens** it, keeping arousal high. People who vent
  stay *angrier*. **Calming down** (reducing arousal) works; venting does not.

## But bottling it also harms
- Chronic **suppression** of anger is linked to **resentment, stress, anxiety, high
  blood pressure and cardiovascular strain**. The healthy path is neither explode nor
  stuff — it's to **process and express it constructively** (assertive, not
  aggressive).

## Handling anger well (stated in the video)
- **Pause and let the 90-second wave pass** before acting (step away, breathe).
- **Name it** ("I'm angry") — labeling an emotion reduces its grip (affect labeling,
  Lieberman).
- **Look underneath** — what's the hurt, fear or need the anger is protecting? Address
  *that*.
- **Be assertive, not aggressive:** express the need/boundary calmly and clearly
  ("I felt… I need…") instead of attacking.
- **Use the energy to fix the problem**, not the person.

## When it's a problem (honest caveat)
- Frequent, intense, or out-of-control anger that damages relationships, work or
  health — or any anger that becomes aggression or abuse toward others — deserves real
  support; anger is very workable in therapy. Not a diagnosis; if your anger scares
  you or others, please reach out.
