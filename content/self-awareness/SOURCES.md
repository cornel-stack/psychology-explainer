# Sources — Every Level of Self-Awareness Explained

A ladder from "blissfully unaware" up to clear, wise self-knowledge — what each level
looks like, the science of why most of us overrate our own self-awareness, and how to
actually climb. Practical, humble, evidence-based. Not a diagnosis.

## The self-awareness paradox
- **Tasha Eurich's research:** about **95% of people believe they're self-aware**, but
  only an estimated **10–15% actually are.** Self-awareness is both widely claimed and
  rare — and, crucially, it's a **skill you can build**, not a fixed trait.

## Level 0 — the blind spot (Dunning–Kruger)
- At the bottom is genuine **unawareness**: you can't see what you can't see. The
  **Dunning–Kruger effect** shows that the least skilled are often the **most
  overconfident**, precisely because the knowledge needed to see your gaps is the same
  knowledge you lack. Blind spots feel like clear sight from the inside.

## The two kinds (internal vs external)
- Eurich distinguishes **internal self-awareness** (clearly seeing your own values,
  emotions, patterns, and reactions) from **external self-awareness** (understanding how
  you **come across to others**). They're **independent** — you can be high on one and
  low on the other — and you need **both**. Many introspective people have almost no
  idea how they land on other people.

## The Johari Window
- The **Johari Window** (Luft & Ingham) maps four zones: the **open** (known to you and
  others), the **hidden** (known to you, not shared), the **blind spot** (others see it,
  you don't), and the **unknown** (nobody sees yet). Growth = shrinking the **blind spot**
  (via feedback) and the **hidden** (via disclosure).

## Metacognition — watching your own mind
- A higher rung is **metacognition**: thinking *about* your thinking. Noticing your
  thoughts, biases, and mental habits as events you can observe, rather than being
  swept along by them unseen. It's the gap between *having* a thought and *noticing* you're
  having it.

## The observing self (defusion)
- Higher still is the **"observing self"** (from mindfulness and **Acceptance &
  Commitment Therapy**): the calm vantage point from which you can watch thoughts and
  feelings pass **without being them**. "I'm having the thought that I'm failing" instead
  of "I'm a failure." This **cognitive defusion** loosens thoughts' grip.

## The introspection trap — ask "what," not "why"
- More navel-gazing doesn't equal more insight. Eurich found that asking **"why"**
  ("why am I like this?") often breeds rumination and false, tidy stories. Asking
  **"what"** ("what am I feeling? what can I do?") leads to **clearer, more useful**
  self-knowledge. **Introspection illusion:** we trust our inner stories more than we
  should.

## Self-acceptance — knowing AND holding it kindly
- The highest form isn't harsh self-scrutiny; it's seeing yourself **clearly and
  accepting yourself** — strengths, flaws, and all. Brutal self-judgement actually
  **blocks** honest self-awareness (we hide from what we'll only punish). True
  self-awareness is paired with **self-compassion**, which makes it safe to keep looking.

## How to climb (stated in the video)
- **Seek honest feedback** — ask trusted people how you come across (shrink the blind
  spot).
- **Ask "what," not "why"** — name feelings and next actions, not endless causes.
- **Practise noticing** — meditation/journaling to watch your mind, not just ride it.
- **Hold your self-story loosely** — you are not your thoughts; stay curious and humble.
- **Pair it with kindness** — you can only face honestly what you won't punish.

## Honest note
- Self-awareness is a **lifelong practice, not a destination** — and even the most aware
  people have blind spots (that humility is itself a sign of it). **Not a diagnosis.**
  The goal isn't relentless self-analysis; it's seeing yourself **clearly, lightly, and
  kindly** enough to grow.
